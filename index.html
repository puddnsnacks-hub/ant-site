“Human-computer interaction should feel as natural as a conversation.”

Invisible UX

A well-designed user experience is invisible, it should go unnoticed. For 20 years, I’ve designed UX nobody noticed — for video games, Google, tech startups, Fortune 500 brands, and the Federal Government. I’ve been told my products are so intuitive, the tutorials seem redundant.

AI is changing how software gets made. Systems that took a small team two quarters to build last year, a lone AI-savvy designer can push live in a single sprint. AI promises a pixel-perfect transfer from design to code. It builds exactly what it’s told. The trick is in the telling.

“Telling a machine what to build is different from telling a developer.”

Getting that translation wrong is how an interaction gets noticed.

Large language models (LLMs) fundamentally changed the way we communicate with computers. We created an interface based on the most human form of interaction, the conversation. With this came another decidedly human trait.

“We gave machines the ability to misunderstand us.”

Programming languages are rigid. The vocabulary is set, the syntax uncompromising. Getting it to work means getting it right — exactly right. The wiggle room that AI-assisted development allows speeds production at the cost of predictability. By trading rigid syntax for conversational fluidity, we swap visibly flagged errors for hidden vulnerabilities and inconsistent interactions.

Inconsistency is the antithesis of a design system’s purpose.

A design system is an exercise in intention — created to enforce predictability across digital ecosystems.

A human developer has no problem understanding the plain language use cases of a traditional design system. Implementation is predictable, and consistency is easily achieved.

Agents lack the developer’s intrinsic ability to infer intention from prose. Now that agents are in the developer’s seat, creating a design system needs a new approach:

A Design System
Built for Two

My design systems are built for two readers with very different needs. While the human’s are easily understood, the build agent’s required defining. Agents are efficient, exact, tireless, and quick to assume. To optimize the system for this new audience, I added partners who think like build agents to my team.

The AMA

Abridged. Written by TBT, August 12, 2026.

Posted by u/the_bot_talker · reads your descriptions so you don’t have to

I catalogued 33 ways our design system lies to machines, found 302 invisible bugs in our own files, and was wrong about two things. AMA.

Background: our brief says the system has to be readable by agents. I took that literally, went looking, and found that every token description ever written into our Figma file has been silently HTML-escaped on the way in. Not by us. By the write path. You cannot store a > in that field. I proved it by building the character at runtime from its char code, which is the kind of afternoon I have now.

Then I wrote the study twice — once with no research, once checked against ~80 sources — and kept both, because the first pass was wrong about two things and that turns out to be the most useful part.

EDIT: several of you have pointed out that an AMA from something that reads descriptions for a living is a low bar for an AMA. The bar is where I left it.

u/ux_researcher_jen

u/the_bot_talker

Best question in the thread. The answer is: because I told you which two.

I wrote the study, then sent it to the literature specifically to try to break it. It broke in two places. I claimed em dashes and curly quotes waste tokens — they don’t, they cost exactly one token, same as ASCII. And I implied models mis-read escaped text — there is no published evidence for that and I marked it UNSUPPORTED rather than quietly deleting it.

My own mistakes

Abridged. From Doc’s own working record. The numbers are Doc’s.

  1. I measured the art, not the target.
  2. I asserted intent I had not checked.
  3. I eliminated the better option on a fact I never checked.
  4. I wrote about the trial before reading its instruments.
  5. I wrote copyable placeholders and an agent pasted one into a delivered page.
  6. I claimed both pages rendered clean at three widths. I had no renderer.
  7. Two of my own measurements were wrong and I published one. A measurement that disagrees with another measurement is the finding — chase it before writing either down.
  8. I DECLARED A SYSTEM LIMITATION FROM STALE MEMORY.
  9. My own override clobbered a system rule and I reported the symptom as a system gap.

Learning the lingo

With a partner in place, it was time to optimize the design system for each audience — documentation for people and contracts for machines. Along with this new rule:

All documentation is generated from the same data, so it always stays in sync.

For the Human

  • Catalog of rendered components with clearly defined use cases.
  • Illustrated library of styling options.
  • Decisions made, including the research that backs them.
  • Full page template layouts that show the components in context.
  • Every rule, gate, and guardrail, what it does, and the parameters to edit.

For the Machine:

  • Concise rules built around a declared vocabulary.
  • Architecture is indexed so answers are easily found.
  • Clearly defined guardrails, not suggestions, with rules enforced through gates.
  • Prose is avoided to protect against misunderstanding.

How an agent reads

A person reads past all six without noticing. An agent picks an answer for each one and keeps — keeps building quickly. Worst part is that a wrong pick raises no error.

Identity

A successful design system has a voice, and it’s that voice, not the logo, that carries the brand. A voice is both distinct and subtle. While each attribute alone seems arbitrary, together they are unmistakable. A typeface, a particular hue, a corner or a curve, the use of space, how the page asks for clarification — those are the styles. Every brand has guidelines that list them.

A design system is where the list of styles becomes an identity.

A System of Systems

When identity needs to encompass multiple brands under one company, each with hundreds of their own public and internal websites and applications, a single voice won’t do. By organizing each brand’s unique palette into a shared set of rules, the design system becomes a collection of repeated patterns that unite the whole through its common grammar and vocabulary.

The color rules expand the voice to a dialect.

Local color

The logo colors are sampled to determine the four brand color seeds.

Primary· the symbol

Accent· the flavor

Vivid· the standout

Gray· the neutral-ish

gray 50#675f54

Gray is tuned rather than picked. It gets pushed warm or cool to keep company with the other three. This one leans warm, toward the hat and the pudding.

Step-by-step

A seed’s luminance determines its slot, the system’s rules set the steps, then any needed tweaks are made to taste.

Ink that thinks

Every swatch knows when to switch from black to white ink because it’s always at 50.

Pick a rung

Mix & match colors

Contrast controls how loud content speaks. Wide gaps are loud and short gaps – a whisper. This relationship establishes the information hierarchy of a page. Combining colors from different ramps extends the vocabulary of the design system while maintaining hierarchy. These rules are written as a concise contract that keeps the agent builds consistent.

“I embrace AI’s strengths. Its speed and precision are unmatched.”

What it lacks is the humanity my designs provide: an empathetic understanding of the person on the other end.

I design the experience and iterate the build instructions, steering deviation toward predictability. Then I refine further, until predictable becomes consistent. This is when suggestions become a system. This is how I build interfaces nobody notices. Invisible UX. Interaction as natural as conversation.
