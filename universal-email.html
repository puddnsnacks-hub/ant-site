Universal Email Design System

Meaning& intent

An email design system for Universal Orlando Resort. The team sent one monthly newsletter in 13 audience versions, and at a glance one send looked like the next. I took the meaning out of the parts. Every block starts blank, and the slot it sits in gives it one of eight jobs.

Client work · Lead designer · November 2025 to February 2026

The cover of the Annual Passholder newsletter, as a layout and with example content for Mardi Gras. Placeholder (FPO) copy and pictures.

The problem

“Everything is a sea of blue.”

Universal’s email team, in the stakeholder interviews

The monthly newsletter alone went out in 13 audience versions per release. The team was asked for more emails, for more audiences, in more looks, and the only tool on hand was another template.

What the team asked for

“Need a flexible system, not hundreds of rigid templates, that will support the various stages of the user’s journey, theme variations, destinations, and purpose.”

Stakeholder interviews, research package

The idea

The slot gives it one

I took the meaning out of the parts. A layout module is an arrangement of elements and nothing more, so the same module can sit in any template, for any destination.

The job arrives with the slot. Each slot in a template is locked to one of eight content categories, and every category answers the same question.

“…those groups form unique layout modules that govern layout behavior and included parts, but have no innate intent. This neutrality allows the same layout modules to be reused across multiple templates and destinations without redefining the module itself.”

“What is this content trying to accomplish?”

Pick a category. (The module stays the same.)

A reviewer can ask what a section is trying to do and check the answer against a written definition. The conversation moves from taste to fact, and the module never has to be redrawn to settle it.

Transaction

“Content whose primary purpose is to confirm, validate, or document a completed guest action. Transactional content is factual, required, and trust-building, and should never feel promotional.”

  • Triggered by a guest action (booking, purchase, registration)
  • Required for clarity and reassurance
  • Focused on accuracy, not persuasion

Entertain

“Content designed to delight, excite, inspire, or emotionally engage the guest without requiring an immediate action or conveying critical information.”

  • Experience-led, not task-driven
  • Optional and additive, never required for completion or compliance
  • Focused on storytelling, mood, or fun rather than instruction or conversion

Inform

“Clearly worded content that educates, offers guidance, or prepares the guest for an upcoming experience.”

  • Not sales-driven
  • Contextual to timing or trip stage
  • Often answers “what should I know?”

Promote

“Content designed to highlight experiences, events, or offerings to generate interest and awareness (discovery, not immediate action).”

  • Experience-led
  • Awareness-focused
  • May be contextual or seasonal

Convert

“Content whose primary goal is to prompt a specific next action. Action-oriented and outcome-driven.”

  • Includes a clear CTA
  • Often time-bound or contextual
  • Supports revenue or engagement goals

Retain

“Content focused on maintaining long-term engagement and loyalty, reinforcing value and belonging.”

  • Relationship-driven
  • Value-reinforcing
  • Often recurring or lifecycle-based

Utility

“Content that provides functional tools or secondary actions supporting the guest experience (not the primary purpose).”

  • Task-oriented
  • Low emotional weight
  • Convenience and usability focused

Compliance

“Content required for legal, regulatory, and operational obligations. Mandatory and standardized.”

  • Required by policy or law
  • Non-negotiable
  • Consistent across sends

My part

What I decided, and why

I was lead designer from November 2025 to February 2026. The work started with research: interviews with Universal’s email team, a consultation with the founder of a customer engagement platform, competitive analysis and an audit of email design systems. Six calls shaped the system, and I wrote the reason for each into the documentation.

  1. Modules carry no meaning. Categories do.

    “This neutrality allows the same layout modules to be reused across multiple templates and destinations without redefining the module itself.”

    Design System OverviewThe idea ↑

  2. A template changes in three ways and no others.

    “A variant keeps the same approved template and module structures, but allows controlled changes through a small set of configurable rules.”

    Template VariantsThree moves ↓

  3. The look sits on top of the structure.

    “A theme is a controlled set of visual and asset rules applied on top of the design system’s structural framework.”

    ThemeTheme ↓

  4. The first cart email does not sell.

    “Stay neutral: Avoid urgency, offers, or persuasion.”

    Abandoned cart · Narrative Strategy & PurposeThe cart series ↓

  5. The saved cart gets a label and no heading.

    “The inventory label is not a content block, it’s a confirmation cue.”

    Cart blueprint · Cart Summary and CTAFour reasons ↓

  6. When the data is in doubt, the line comes out.

    “Omit optional lines rather than guessing specifics”

    Annual Passholder newsletter blueprint · Cross-Block DefaultsOne line ↓

How it flexes

Three moves, and no fourth

A template changes in three ways: hide an optional element, hide an optional module, or repeat a group. Dashed borders mark what is optional, so the choice is visible before anyone makes it.

“Variants are the system’s way to adapt a base layout to different scenarios without creating a new layout.”

Theme

Same letter, new colors

Theme changes the logo, the color and the pictures. The layout under it stays where it is, so a send can look like its park, its event or its season without becoming a new template.

Theme may influence:

  • Destination logo instance within a fixed logo container
  • Color tokens (backgrounds, accents, dividers, CTA styles where permitted)
  • Imagery and graphic assets (hero/background images, icons, overlays/pop-flats)
  • Approved decorative treatments (only when defined as part of theme rules)

Theme can be derived from:

Destination, Park, Land, Attraction, Event, Season

The system at work

Remember, reconsider, decide

A guest who leaves a trip in the cart gets three emails, and each one is allowed to say one more thing than the last. The first carries one button and no offer. Urgency waits for the third, and it has to be true.

“The guest is guided, not rushed, through a decision they were already considering.”

Four reasons to leave out one heading

The cart reminder shows what the guest saved under a short label and nothing else. I gave a column of that page to explaining why the label gets no heading above it.

  1. 1

    “A heading adds friction at the wrong moment”

    “At Touchpoint 1, the goal is recognition and reassurance, not explanation.”

  2. 2

    “The message already sets the context”

    “Adding a heading would repeat context that’s already been established and risk making the email feel system-generated.”

  3. 3

    “Inventory labels work best as inline reinforcement”

    “The inventory label is not a content block, it’s a confirmation cue.”

  4. 4

    “Headings push the email toward ‘transactional’”

    “They signal structure and finality. A cart reminder should feel open-ended and flexible, not procedural.”
